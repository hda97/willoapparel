# 2. Character Design (from *Character*, 2021)

> **Architecture & Scope Notice:**  
> - **[Core McKee Pentalogy Framework]:** Synthesizes Robert McKee's masterwork *Character: The Art of Role and Cast Design for Page, Stage, and Screen* (2021), specifically: Characterization vs. True Character, dimensionality as contradiction, the four concentric realms of self, and cast design as a gravitational solar system.  
> - **[Modern Pedagogical Case Studies & Extensions]:** Specific television and film character mappings (e.g., Walter White from *Breaking Bad*, Tony Soprano from *The Sopranos*) and the 5-Axis Contradiction Grid template are pedagogical operationalizations created by the engine author.

## 1. Characterization vs. True Character

The single greatest mistake in character creation is confusing **Characterization** with **True Character**.

- **Characterization (The Outer Mask):** Everything observable in five minutes of watching or reading—age, IQ, speech rhythm, job, wardrobe, scars, sociability, nervous tics, stated beliefs.
- **True Character (The Inner Essence):** Who this person *actually is* at the core when every social mask is stripped away. **True Character can only be expressed through choice under dilemma.** The greater the pressure, the deeper the revelation.

### The Core Law of Revelation
If a character appears to be a gentle, devoted family man (*Characterization*), and under crisis chooses to be a gentle, devoted family man (*True Character*), **you have a flat, zero-dimensional character**.
Revelation happens only when the choice under pressure **contradicts** or complicates the outer mask.

---

## 2. What a "Dimension" Actually Is

Writers often say a character is "three-dimensional" because they are "ambitious, funny, and a great cook." According to McKee, **those are three flat traits, not dimensions.**

> **McKee's Law of Dimensionality:** A dimension is a **consistent contradiction**—either between outer Characterization and inner True Character, or within the inner self itself (Conscious Desire vs. Subconscious Need).

### Building a Multi-Dimensional Protagonist (The 4–6 Axis Grid)
To create a living, unforgettable protagonist (on the level of Walter White, Tony Soprano, Odysseus, or Elizabeth I), construct at least **3 to 5 Axes of Contradiction**:

| Axis Type | Surface Pole (Mask / Conscious) | Deep Pole (Shadow / Under Pressure) | How It Explodes in Action |
|-----------|---------------------------------|-------------------------------------|---------------------------|
| **1. Morality Axis** | Principled / Empathetic protector | Ruthless / Self-preserving predator | Sacrifices an innocent to protect their "ideal" of family. |
| **2. Power Axis** | Humble, conflict-avoidant mediator | Secretly tyrannical / Craves absolute control | Uses guilt and martyrdom as weapons to dominate the room. |
| **3. Intellect/Truth Axis** | Hyper-rational scientist / Truth-seeker | Deeply superstitious / Self-deceiving | Rationalizes a catastrophic lie using cold scientific logic. |
| **4. Intimacy Axis** | Charismatic, warm, vulnerable lover | Terrified of being truly seen / Saboteur | Provokes a betrayal the moment someone gets too close. |
| **5. Identity Axis** | Content with a modest, ordinary life | Consumed by envy of their unlived potential | Builds a machine—or burns down their life—to steal another path. |

## 3. The Four Selves of a Human Being

Every rich character operates across four concentric realms of selfhood:

1. **The Public Self (Persona):** The formal role presented to strangers, institutions, and society.
2. **The Personal Self:** The warmer, more relaxed mask shown to friends, family, and lovers.
3. **The Private Self (Conscious Inner Life):** What the character knows and admits only to themselves when the door is locked (their conscious secrets, calculations, and anxieties).
4. **The Hidden / Subconscious Self (The Shadow):** Desires, terrors, and wounds the character **cannot even admit to themselves**—forces that erupt only when extreme pressure cracks the first three layers.

---

## 4. Conscious Desire vs. Subconscious Need

- **One-Dimensional Characters** want only one thing, and they know exactly what it is.
- **Complex Characters** are split in two:
  - **Conscious Desire (The Object of Desire):** The external, tangible goal they pursue throughout the plot (*"I must win the Nobel Prize / build the Box / reclaim my stolen life"*).
  - **Subconscious Need (The Contradictory Drive):** The deeper psychological truth they are fleeing or starving for, which often stands in **direct opposition** to their conscious goal (*"I need to forgive myself for being ordinary and love the imperfect life I already created"*).

In great drama, the Climax forces a collision where the protagonist can only fulfill their **Subconscious Need** by sacrificing or transforming their **Conscious Desire** (or vice versa in tragedy).

---

## 5. Cast Design as a Solar System

Never invent secondary characters in isolation. A cast is a **gravitational system**:

- **The Protagonist is the Sun:** The center of dramatic gravity.
- **Major Supporting Characters are Planets:** Each planet exists for a specific structural purpose—to exert gravitational pull on a **different contradiction axis** of the protagonist, forcing hidden dimensions into the light:
  - *Planet A (The Lover/Partner):* Exposes the protagonist's Intimacy vs. Control contradiction.
  - *Planet B (The Foil / Double / Rival):* Embodies the extreme path the protagonist *could* have taken, acting as a living mirror of their Shadow.
  - *Planet C (The Ally / Mentor):* Pressures their Morality vs. Ambition axis.
- **Bit Players are Satellites/Moons:** Brief encounters (a bartender, a guard, a bureaucrat) given **one sharp, unexpected trait** to spark immediate friction in a single scene rather than serving as robotic plot dispensers.

---

## 6. Dramatic vs. Comic Character Architecture

- **Dramatic Character:** Defined by **psychological flexibility and awareness**. When reality strikes back across The Gap, the dramatic character suffers, adapts, questions themselves, and transforms.
- **Comic Character:** Defined by a **Blind Obsession (Manic Fixation)**. A comic character has a massive blind spot they cannot see (arrogance, pedantry, hypochondria, status-mania). When the world hits them with humiliation, they do not learn—they double down on their delusion with even greater conviction.
$> \text{Comic Formula: } \text{Normal Persona} + \text{One Blind Obsession} \times \text{Escalating Social Collision}$.
