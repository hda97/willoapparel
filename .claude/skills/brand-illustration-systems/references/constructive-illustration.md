# Constructive illustration: from subject to branded finish

This method is the common spine beneath every visual family. It explains how to make the subject correct before making
it stylistically distinctive. Use it for sketches, SVG paths, hand drawing, 3D blockouts, collage, or generated-image
art direction.

The stages are cumulative:

`subject model → composition → skeleton → masses → volume → clean form → brand surface → finish → reduction`

Do not solve a failure in an early stage with decoration from a later one.

## 1. Model the subject

Write a compact subject model before drawing:

- **noun:** what it is;
- **verb:** what it is doing or changing;
- **essential parts:** the parts recognition or function depends on;
- **axes and joints:** centre lines, hinges, pivots, wheel hubs, folds, or symmetry;
- **contacts:** what touches, supports, holds, carries, sits on, or passes behind what;
- **constraints:** proportions, anatomy, mechanics, perspective, safety, or cultural accuracy;
- **recognition test:** the one read that must survive at minimum size.

Collect references for anatomy, mechanisms, clothing, objects, and poses as needed. A style reference cannot substitute
for subject reference.

## 2. Compose the action

Make two or three tiny thumbnails before detail. Establish:

- dominant action line or flow path;
- focal point and secondary read;
- crop, scale, ground, and horizon;
- large positive and negative shapes;
- location of copy, UI, controls, or safe areas;
- whether movement travels across, toward, or away from the viewer.

Prefer one large movement over many unrelated accents. A composition should explain where the eye enters, travels,
and stops.

## 3. Build the skeleton

Draw functional guides only. Use the guide appropriate to the subject:

- **person or animal:** head, ribcage, pelvis, spine/action line, shoulders/hips, joints, hands/feet, gaze;
- **vehicle or machine:** wheel/shaft centres, chassis/frame, load path, steering or moving parts, contact with ground;
- **product or object:** bounding box, central axis, symmetry, main planes, openings, handles, functional junctions;
- **scene:** ground/horizon, perspective or projection grid, subject anchors, interaction lines, depth bands;
- **abstract process:** start/end states, actors, containers, transfer path, stages, cause-and-effect arrows;
- **character/device:** base proportions, pivot points, expression axes, silhouette changes between states.

Resolve proportions and contacts here. If guides are wrong, every later layer will be wrong more expensively.

## 4. Block the masses

Turn guides into a small vocabulary of primitives: circles, ellipses, boxes, polygons, cylinders, cones, ribbons, or
cut-paper planes. Use as few as possible while retaining the essential read.

Check:

- black silhouette recognition;
- clear negative spaces at joints and overlaps;
- foreground/background separation;
- no accidental tangencies;
- stable balance or intentionally unstable motion;
- enough room for the intended crop and copy.

For a patterned or detailed family, first make a completely unpatterned version. If it fails, fix the masses.

## 5. Add volume, planes, and occlusion

Choose a depth model and keep it consistent:

- flat overlap only;
- frontal with in-plane lean;
- orthographic or isometric projection;
- oblique extrusion;
- linear perspective;
- modelled 3D camera and light.

Define which parts pass in front, behind, through, or around others. Add cylinders around limbs, tubes around frames,
front/side/top planes, folds around joints, or extrusion along one vector. Foreshortening must follow the selected
projection rather than changing from part to part.

## 6. Refine and clean the form

Add details in order of information value:

1. identity and expression;
2. functional parts;
3. clothing, material, or local structure;
4. decorative detail.

Clean paths and define the system's corner radii, terminals, stroke ratios, joins, and tapered contours. Remove details
that disappear at intended size or compete with the focal action. Keep construction guides on a hidden or labelled layer
when the source format supports it.

## 7. Apply the brand grammar

Map the selected direction onto the constructed subject:

- **colour roles:** field, subject, structure, depth, signal, quiet texture;
- **shape language:** round/angled, taut/soft, modular/organic, symmetrical/asymmetrical;
- **line:** weight, taper, rhythm, gaps, outline ownership;
- **pattern:** owner, scale, direction, density, contrast, clipping, reduced state;
- **material:** paper, ink, clay, metal, glass, fabric, light, or intentionally immaterial shape;
- **device:** frame, portal, connector, annotation, character, or transformation rule;
- **composition:** repeated scale, crop, balance, and safe-area rules.

Pattern belongs to named surfaces or roles. It follows the geometry of that owner—or deliberately remains screen-fixed
when the rule says so—and never floats indiscriminately across the image.

## 8. Colour, light, and motion

Establish value hierarchy before hue. Use a small number of colour roles rather than many equal colours. Verify that
the focal subject survives grayscale and colour-vision constraints.

Light is optional, not an automatic polish layer. A flat family may use no light at all. A dimensional family must
define source direction, softness, material response, ambient fill, and shadow rules.

Motion must express a verb from the idea or subject: connect, deposit, orbit, unfold, stretch, settle, reveal. Animate
cause and effect, not random bobbing. Specify what remains fixed, timing, easing, loop behaviour, and reduced state.

## 9. Reduce and verify

Inspect these gates in order:

1. **Structure:** anatomy/mechanics, axes, joints, contacts, proportions.
2. **Composition:** action path, hierarchy, crop, copy/UI relationship.
3. **Silhouette:** recognisable as one black shape at intended size.
4. **Occlusion:** front/back order and projection are unambiguous.
5. **Value:** primary and secondary reads survive grayscale.
6. **Brand:** ownership survives without the logo and primary colour.
7. **Surface:** patterns and details stay subordinate and obey their owners.
8. **Production:** paths, layers, tokens, camera, materials, and motion remain editable.
9. **Reduction:** micro and reduced-motion versions retain meaning.

Fix the earliest failed gate before proceeding.

## Worked construction: a human riding a bicycle

### A. Bicycle foundation

1. Draw two equal wheel circles and mark both hubs.
2. Place the bottom bracket between them, usually slightly below the hub line.
3. Connect rear hub, bottom bracket, seat tube, head tube, and front hub into the bike's frame and fork.
4. Add handlebar and saddle anchors. Confirm wheelbase, frame clearance, and ground contacts.

### B. Rider skeleton

1. Fix the three contact systems: pelvis on saddle, hands on handlebars, feet on pedals.
2. Place pelvis and ribcage masses, connect them with a leaning spine/action line, then place head and gaze.
3. Connect shoulders to hands through elbows and hips to pedals through knees. One leg extends as its pedal descends;
   the other bends more sharply.
4. Check balance: the rider's centre of mass, reach, and knee path must be plausible for the bike geometry.

### C. Volume and overlap

1. Turn torso and pelvis into masses; arms and legs into tapered cylinders.
2. Thicken frame lines into tubes, tyres into rings, and saddle/handlebars/pedals into functional forms.
3. Resolve which limbs and bike parts overlap. Preserve negative gaps around elbows, knees, and frame openings.
4. Test the combined rider-and-bike silhouette before details.

### D. Refinement and brand application

1. Add clothing around the body masses, then hair, shoes, hands, face, chain, crank, brakes, and only the spokes needed.
2. Apply the chosen family's shape, line, colour, pattern, and material rules to named planes or garments.
3. Use pattern to describe form or ownership, not to conceal anatomy.

### E. Light and motion

1. Add light and cast shadow only if the direction calls for them.
2. Show motion through lean, trailing shapes, wheel treatment, fabric response, road relationship, or a controlled
   movement path. Do not rely on decorative speed lines to make a static pose dynamic.
3. Make a reduced version that preserves wheels, rider gesture, and the three contact systems.

## How the same spine changes by family

- **Flat spot:** primitives become clean planes; overlap and value replace modelling.
- **Character-led:** skeleton, pose, hands, gaze, and expression carry meaning; model sheets preserve proportions.
- **Expressive line:** gesture may remain visible; line pressure and omission replace filled volume.
- **Soft 3D:** masses become a blockout, then topology/material/light; silhouette and camera are still solved first.
- **Pattern-led:** pattern appears after silhouette and plane ownership; density reduces by tier.
- **Technical:** guides become the visible grid, projection, connectors, labels, and exploded states.
- **Brand device:** one repeated transformation or frame acts on correctly built subjects.
- **Collage:** masses become cutouts; scale, edge treatment, and overlap replace drawn volume.

The construction method stays stable while the visible result changes completely.
